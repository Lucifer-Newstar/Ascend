## Gaining weight when you are underweight
To gain weight you need to eat more energy than you use, every day, for weeks. Add a snack or two (milk, curd, banana, peanuts, eggs, sundal), bigger portions of rice or chapati at main meals, and some healthy fat (a spoon of ghee, nuts). Strength training makes sure much of the gain is muscle. Aim for slow, steady gain and weigh yourself weekly. If you are very underweight or cannot gain, see a doctor first.

## How fast to lose or gain weight
Slow changes are easier to keep and preserve more muscle. Many guidelines suggest losing about 0.5 to 1 kg a week at most, and lean gaining at roughly 0.25 to 0.5 kg a week. ASCEND's goal check warns you when a goal needs a faster pace than that.

## How much protein you need
For people who train, sports-nutrition position stands (for example the International Society of Sports Nutrition, 2017) commonly recommend about 1.4 to 2.0 g of protein per kg of body weight per day, spread over 3 to 5 meals. A 50 kg person would aim for roughly 70 to 100 g a day. Whole foods can cover this; powders are only a convenience.

## Protein in a vegetarian Indian diet
Vegetarians can reach their protein target with dal and sambar (lentils), chana and rajma, sundal, curd and milk, paneer, soya chunks, peanuts and other nuts, and eggs if you eat them. Combining cereals with pulses (rice with dal, idli with sambar) improves protein quality. Add one protein food to every meal.

## Eating before a workout
A normal meal 2 to 3 hours before training, or a small snack 30 to 60 minutes before (a banana, a couple of idlis, curd, bread with peanut butter), gives energy without a heavy stomach. Training on an empty stomach is fine for some people; if you feel weak or dizzy, eat something first.

## Eating after a workout
There is no strict window: a meal with protein and carbohydrate within a few hours of training supports recovery. Rice with dal or egg curry, curd rice, chapati with paneer or a glass of milk all work.

## Creatine
Creatine monohydrate is one of the most studied supplements. Sports-nutrition position stands describe it as safe and effective for healthy adults doing strength or high-intensity training, usually taken at about 3 to 5 g a day. It is not needed to make progress. Under 18, or with kidney problems, ask a doctor first.

## Supplements in general
Supplements only fill gaps: food, sleep and consistent training matter far more. If you use any, prefer simple products with third-party testing, avoid "fat burners" and anything promising fast results, and check with a doctor if you take medicine or are under 18.

## Water and hydration
Drink regularly through the day and a little more around training and in hot weather. Pale yellow urine is a simple sign you are drinking enough. Thirst, headaches and dark urine are signs to drink more.

## Eating late at night
For weight change, the total you eat across the day matters much more than the clock. A late dinner does not cause weight gain on its own, but late heavy meals can disturb sleep for some people.

## Skipping breakfast
Breakfast is not required for health or weight control; what matters is the total and quality of what you eat across the day. If you train in the morning or find yourself overeating later, a light breakfast helps. If you are trying to gain weight, skipping meals makes it harder.

## Healthy snacks
Good snacks give protein or fibre: sundal, roasted chana, peanuts, a boiled egg, curd or buttermilk, fruit with a few nuts, sprouts salad, or a glass of milk. They help most when you are trying to reach a protein or calorie target.

## Reading calories and macros
Protein and carbohydrate give about 4 kcal per gram and fat about 9 kcal per gram (alcohol about 7). Label and table values are estimates: home recipes differ a lot with oil and portion size, so treat any number as approximate.
