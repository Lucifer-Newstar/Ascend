## Breathing and bracing when lifting
For heavy squats, deadlifts and presses, take a big breath into the belly before the rep, brace the stomach as if about to be poked, then breathe out after the hardest part or at the top. Holding the breath briefly (the Valsalva manoeuvre) raises blood pressure a lot, so people with high blood pressure, heart or eye problems should ask a doctor and breathe out during the effort instead. For lighter sets, breathe out as you push.

## Breathing on the bench press
Take a breath and set your upper back before unracking, keep it as the bar comes down, and breathe out as you press or at the top. Between reps, take a fresh breath at the top while the arms are locked. Always use a spotter or safety arms for heavy sets.

## Lifting belts and straps
A belt gives the stomach something to brace against and may help on near-maximal squats and deadlifts; it is not needed for most training and does not replace good bracing. Straps help the grip on heavy pulls when the back is not the limit, but train the grip without them too. Neither is needed as a beginner.

## Shoes for lifting
Flat, firm shoes (or bare feet where allowed) keep you stable for deadlifts. Soft, cushioned running shoes can feel wobbly under a heavy squat. Weightlifting shoes with a raised heel help some people squat deeper with an upright chest. Choose what keeps you stable and pain-free.

## Training with only dumbbells
Dumbbells cover a full body: goblet squats, split squats, dumbbell Romanian deadlifts, floor or bench presses, one-arm rows (with a hand on a chair or bench, or bent over without support), overhead presses and curls. Use slower reps and more reps when the dumbbells feel light.

## Deadlifts with dumbbells
Stand with the dumbbells in front of or beside the thighs. Push the hips back with a flat back and soft knees, lower the weights along the legs to mid-shin, then drive the hips forward to stand tall. This dumbbell Romanian deadlift trains the hamstrings, glutes and back. Keep the weights close to the legs.

## Machines or free weights
Both build muscle. Machines are easier to learn and safer to push close to failure; free weights train balance and stabilising muscles and are used in strength sports. A mix works well. Choose exercises you can do with good form and progress over time.

## Training only the upper or lower body
Training only one half leaves the other half weaker and can unbalance posture and sports performance. Train the whole body over the week: legs, pushing, pulling and core. If one area is injured, train the rest and follow a doctor's or physiotherapist's advice for the rest.

## Using a hand gripper safely
Start with a gripper you can close for 8 to 15 reps. Train grip two or three times a week, not every day at full effort, because tendons in the fingers recover slowly. Stop if the finger joints, palm or wrist become painful rather than just tired. Dead hangs and farmer's carries also build grip. ASCEND has a Hand Gripper Squeeze entry.

## Kettlebell swing technique
The swing is a hip hinge: hike the bell back between the thighs with a flat back, then snap the hips forward so the bell floats to chest height, the arms only guiding it. Do not squat it up or lift it with the shoulders. Start with a light bell and short sets. ASCEND has a Kettlebell Swing entry.

## Trap bar or straight bar deadlift
The trap (hex) bar puts the weight at your sides, so the back stays more upright and the legs do more; many beginners find it easier to learn and kinder to the lower back. The straight bar is the competition lift. Both build strength; pick the one you can do with good form.

## Choosing your starting weight
Pick a weight you can lift for the target reps with good form and about two reps still in the tank. If the last reps get slow and ugly, it is too heavy; if you could do many more, go up next set. ASCEND's progression suggestions build on what you log.

## Spotters and safety bars
For heavy bench press and squats, set the safety arms of the rack at a height just below the bottom of your rep, or ask a spotter who knows how to help. Never bench heavy alone without safety arms, and learn to bail a squat safely with an empty bar first.

## Front squat or back squat
Both are good. The front squat keeps the chest upright and works the thighs and upper back more; the back squat usually allows more weight. Wrist or shoulder stiffness can make the front rack hard; a cross-arm grip or straps help. Choose the one you can do with a full, pain-free range.
