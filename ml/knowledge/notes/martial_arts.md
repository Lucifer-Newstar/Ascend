## Starting boxing or Muay Thai at home
You can learn the basics solo: stance and guard, footwork, the jab, cross, hook and uppercut, simple kicks, and shadowboxing in rounds of 2 to 3 minutes with a minute of rest. Use a mirror or film yourself. ASCEND's martial-arts drills are all solo, in the air or on a bag. A class with a qualified coach is the best next step, and anything with a partner, sparring or grappling belongs there, not at home.

## Boxing stance: orthodox or southpaw
Orthodox means the left foot and left hand lead, usual for right-handers; southpaw is the mirror, usual for left-handers. Stand side-on with feet about shoulder-width, weight balanced, knees soft, hands up by the cheeks and chin tucked. Pick the stance that feels natural for your strong hand at the back; you can learn the other side later.

## How to throw a hook
The hook is a curved punch with the elbow bent near 90 degrees at shoulder height. The power comes from turning the hip and pivoting the lead foot, not from swinging the arm. Keep the fist, elbow and shoulder moving as one, the other hand guarding your chin, and bring the hand straight back to your cheek. Practise slowly in the air before hitting a bag. ASCEND has a Hook Shadow Drill.

## How to throw an uppercut
The uppercut rises from below: dip a little at the knees towards the punching side, then drive up with the legs and turn the hip, the palm facing you. Keep it short, the elbow close to your body, and return to guard at once. A big wind-up leaves you open. ASCEND has an Uppercut Shadow Drill.

## The jab and cross (one-two)
The jab is the quick straight punch with the lead hand; the cross is the straight punch with the rear hand, powered by turning the back hip and heel. Snap each punch out and straight back to your cheek, breathe out on every punch, and keep the elbows from flaring. Do not lock the elbow hard when punching air. ASCEND has a Jab-Cross Shadow Drill.

## Muay Thai elbows and knees
Elbows are short-range strikes: turn the hips and shoulders and slice the elbow across or upward with the hand relaxed and the forearm protecting your face. Knees drive forward and up from the hips, often from a clinch. Practise both slowly in the air or on a bag; never on a partner without a coach. ASCEND has Elbow Strike and Knee Strike drills.

## Teep (push kick) and roundhouse kick basics
The teep is a straight push with the ball of the foot or the heel, used to keep distance: lift the knee, then push the hips forward. The roundhouse swings the shin round as the hip turns over and the standing foot pivots. For both, lift the knee first, keep the guard up and land back in stance. Speed and height come after balance. ASCEND has Teep and Roundhouse drills.

## Checking kicks
Checking means blocking a low kick with your raised shin: lift the knee up and slightly out so the shin meets the kick, with the same-side elbow down near the knee. It needs good balance on one leg. Practise the movement solo; checking real kicks belongs in a class with a coach and controlled partners. ASCEND has a Kick Check Drill.

## Boxing defence: guard, parry, block and slip
Defence starts with the guard: hands up, elbows in, chin down. A parry is a small tap that redirects a straight punch; a block catches a hook on the glove and forearm; a slip moves the head off the line of a straight punch by turning slightly; bob and weave ducks under hooks with the legs, not by bending the back. Practise solo, slowly, mixed with your own punches. ASCEND has Parry and Block and Slip and Roll drills.

## Footwork and speed
Good footwork keeps you balanced: step with the foot closest to where you are going first, slide the other after it, and never cross your feet. Practise steps, pivots and angles in short rounds, light on the balls of the feet. Speed comes from staying relaxed, a short path for each strike, and practice; skipping and short sprints help foot speed.

## Hand wraps and gloves
Hand wraps support the wrist and the small bones of the hand and protect the knuckles; wrap before every bag or pad session. Bag gloves around 12 to 16 oz are common for adults, with heavier gloves usually used for sparring. Hitting a heavy bag with bare hands often injures the knuckles and wrists.

## Heavy bag training for fitness
Bag work is good cardio and builds power and timing. Work in rounds (for example 3 to 6 rounds of 2 to 3 minutes with a minute of rest), throw relaxed combinations, move between them and breathe out on every strike. Start with light punches until your wrists and shoulders are used to it. On its own it does not train the legs much, so keep some leg strength work.

## Shadowboxing
Shadowboxing is fighting an imagined opponent in the air. It trains technique, footwork, defence and conditioning with no equipment. Move your feet, mix punches with defence, and keep the guard up between strikes. Do not snap the elbows to full lockout on empty air. Rounds of 2 to 3 minutes are a good start.

## Conditioning shins and knuckles
Shins get used to kicking by regular, gradual kicking on a heavy bag over months. Hitting hard objects like poles, rolling bottles over the shin, or punching walls to harden bones and knuckles risks fractures, nerve damage and long-term joint problems; there is no need for it. Bruises should heal before the next hard session, and pain at a single spot on the bone needs a doctor.

## Sparring safely
Sparring should only happen in a gym with a qualified coach, with a mouthguard, the right gloves and headgear where used, and a partner who agrees on a light pace. Beginners learn more from light, technical sparring than from hard rounds. Stop and get medical advice after any blow that causes headache, dizziness, confusion, sickness or blurred vision: these can be signs of concussion.

## Kicking higher
High kicks need hip mobility, strength to lift the leg, and balance. Warm up with leg swings, practise slow controlled kicks holding a wall, and work on flexibility with stretches such as the pancake, front split practice and hip flexor stretches after training. Lift the knee high first; height comes over months. Never force a kick into pain.

## Breakfalls (falling safely)
Breakfalls teach you to fall without hitting the head or landing on an arm: tuck the chin, round the back, and slap the mat with the arms to spread the impact. Learn them only on a thick mat, starting from sitting and then from a squat. Falls from standing, rolls and throws should be learned in a class with a coach.

## Horse stance
A horse stance is a wide, low stance with the feet parallel, the knees pushed out over the toes and the back upright. Holding it builds leg endurance and is used in many traditional styles. Start with short holds of 20 to 30 seconds at a comfortable depth and build up; knee pain means come up higher. ASCEND has a Horse Stance Hold.

## Martial arts and strength training in the same week
You can do both. Put strength sessions on separate days from hard martial arts training where you can, or lift after skill work so technique is practised fresh. Two or three strength sessions a week focusing on legs, pulling, pushing and core support striking. Keep at least one full rest day and eat enough to recover.

## Self-defence basics
Most self-defence is awareness: noticing trouble early, avoiding it, de-escalating, and getting away to a safe place and help. Physical techniques need practice with a qualified instructor and partners to work under stress; a few videos are not enough. ASCEND only covers solo fitness drills, not fighting techniques.

## Kata and forms practice
Kata (forms) are set sequences of techniques. Learn them from your instructor, practise slowly with correct stances first, then add speed and power. Breathing out on strikes and finishing where you started are common checks. Short daily practice helps more than one long session a week. Log the time in ASCEND as Karate Kata.
