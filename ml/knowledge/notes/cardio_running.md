## How hard should cardio feel (the talk test)
A simple way to judge effort: during moderate cardio you can talk but not sing; during vigorous cardio you can only say a few words before taking a breath. The US Physical Activity Guidelines (2018) use this talk test. Most of your weekly cardio can be at the easy, conversational end, with a smaller share of hard work. Easy sessions build the base; hard sessions are the extra on top.

## Zone 2 and easy runs
Zone 2 is the popular name for easy, steady cardio: breathing is deeper than at rest, but you can still hold a conversation. Easy runs, brisk walks or cycling at this pace build endurance with little wear and are easy to recover from. A heart rate monitor is optional; the talk test works. Many runners keep most of their weekly running easy and only one or two sessions hard.

## Maximum heart rate and heart rate zones
The formula 220 minus your age only gives a rough estimate of maximum heart rate; real values for people of the same age differ by 10 or more beats a minute. Use heart rate zones as a guide, not a rule, and check them against how the effort feels. If your heart rate seems unusually high or irregular, or you feel chest pain or faint, stop and get it checked by a doctor.

## Starting to run as a beginner (run-walk, couch to 5K)
Start with run-walk intervals: for example 1 minute of easy jogging and 2 minutes of walking, 20 to 30 minutes, three times a week, with a rest day between. Every week, jog a little longer and walk a little less. Programs like couch to 5K follow this idea and take about 8 to 10 weeks. Run slowly enough to talk; finishing fresh beats finishing broken. ASCEND has a Run-Walk Intervals entry to log it.

## How fast to increase running distance
Increase weekly distance or time gradually. A common rule of thumb is no more than about 10% more per week, with an easier week every few weeks; it is a rule of thumb, not a proven limit. Sudden jumps in distance, speed or hills are a common reason runners get sore shins, knees or feet. Sleep, food and rest days are part of the plan.

## HIIT and interval training
High-intensity interval training alternates short hard efforts (20 seconds to a few minutes) with easy recovery. It improves fitness in less time than steady cardio, but it is tiring: one to three sessions a week is plenty, on days apart from heavy leg training. Warm up first, choose moves you can do with good form while tired, and step instead of jump if your knees or shins complain. Beginners should build a few weeks of regular easy cardio first.

## Tabata workouts
A Tabata is 8 rounds of 20 seconds of very hard work and 10 seconds of rest, 4 minutes in total, usually on a bike, a rower or with simple bodyweight moves. Warm up for 5 to 10 minutes first and cool down afterwards. It is short but hard; once or twice a week is enough, and good form matters more than the rep count.

## Sprint training safely
Sprints build speed and power. Warm up for 10 minutes, then do a few build-up runs before any all-out effort. Keep sprints short (10 to 30 seconds), rest fully between them, and stop the session when your speed drops. Hill sprints are kinder to the hamstrings than flat sprints for beginners. A sudden sharp pain at the back of the thigh means stop straight away.

## Shin splints
If your shins hurt when you run, it is often shin splints: pain along the inner edge of the shin bone during or after running. It is usually linked to doing too much too soon. Reduce running, switch to cycling or swimming for a while, and build back gradually. Pain at one small spot on the bone, pain at rest or at night, or pain that does not improve needs a doctor or physiotherapist to rule out a stress fracture.

## Side stitch while running
A stitch is a sharp pain under the ribs during exercise. Slow down, breathe deeply and evenly, and press on the spot or stretch the arm on that side overhead. Avoiding a large meal or lots of drink in the hour before running helps many people. If chest pain spreads to the arm, jaw or back, or comes with breathlessness, stop and get medical help.

## Knee pain when running
Mild stiffness that eases after a few minutes of warm-up is common, but knee pain that gets worse as you run, swelling, clicking with pain, or the knee giving way is not normal. Rest from running, try low-impact cardio, and see a doctor or physiotherapist if it lasts more than a few days. Gradual progress, strong legs and hips, and suitable shoes help prevent it.

## Cardio that is easy on the knees
Low-impact options load the joints less than running or jumping: cycling or a stationary bike, swimming, the elliptical trainer, rowing, and brisk walking (flat or uphill). They count towards the weekly cardio target the same way. If cardio still causes knee pain, see a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Breathing while running
There is no single correct way. Breathe through the mouth and nose together when the effort is hard; many runners settle into a rhythm such as breathing in for two or three steps and out for two or three steps. If you cannot catch your breath, slow down: running a little slower usually fixes it. Breathlessness that is unusual for you, wheezing or chest pain needs a doctor.

## Breathing when swimming freestyle
Breathe out steadily into the water through the nose and mouth while your face is down, then turn the head to the side with the stroke and take a quick breath in through the mouth. Holding your breath under water is what makes beginners run out of air. Many swimmers breathe every 2 or 3 strokes. Practise with a kickboard and short lengths first.

## Running in hot weather
Heat makes the same pace feel harder. Run early in the morning or in the evening, slow down, drink water before and after, and add some salt or an electrolyte drink on long sweaty sessions. Wear light clothing. Stop and cool down if you feel dizzy, confused, sick or stop sweating: these can be signs of heat illness and need urgent help if they do not settle quickly.

## Barefoot or forefoot running
Switching to minimal shoes or landing on the forefoot loads the calves and Achilles tendons much more. Change slowly over weeks to months, starting with a few minutes per run, and expect sore calves at first. A painful Achilles tendon is a sign to back off. There is no strong evidence that one footstrike is best for everyone.

## Cardio while trying to gain weight
Cardio is good for the heart even when you are building up. Keep it moderate (for example two or three easy 20 to 30 minute sessions a week, or daily walking), do it after or apart from strength training, and eat a little extra to cover it. Very long cardio sessions make gaining weight harder.

## Building stamina for sports
Stamina comes from regular easy cardio (to build the base) plus intervals that copy the sport: short sprints with short rest for football, rounds of 2 to 3 minutes for boxing. Build up over weeks, keep one or two hard sessions a week, and add strength training for legs and core. Sleep and food decide how well you recover between sessions.
