## Whey protein before or after a workout
Total protein across the day matters much more than exact timing. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN, 2017) suggests spreading protein over meals every few hours and having some within a few hours of training. A shake before or after training both work; whole foods work too.

## Mass gainers
A mass gainer is a powder of carbohydrates and protein that adds a lot of calories. It can help if you struggle to eat enough, but milk, bananas, peanut butter, oats or dates and curd do the same job, often for less money and with fewer added sugars. Check the label for sugar and serving size.

## Caffeine before training
Caffeine can improve endurance and strength performance. The ISSN position stand (2021) uses about 3 to 6 mg per kg of body weight about an hour before exercise; many people feel the effect with less, such as a cup of coffee. Too much causes jitters, a racing heart and poor sleep; avoid it late in the day. Under 18, energy drinks and pre-workouts are best avoided.

## Pre-workout supplements
Pre-workout powders are usually caffeine plus other ingredients; the caffeine does most of the work. Check the caffeine dose on the label, do not stack it with coffee or energy drinks, and avoid products with undisclosed 'proprietary blends'. They are not needed to train well.

## Beta-alanine
Beta-alanine may help hard efforts lasting about 1 to 4 minutes, according to the ISSN position stand (2015). It commonly causes a harmless tingling of the skin; splitting the daily dose reduces it. It is not needed for most beginners.

## BCAAs
Branched-chain amino acids are already in protein foods and whey. If you eat enough protein through the day, extra BCAAs add little for muscle growth. Spend the money on food instead.

## Ashwagandha
Ashwagandha is a herb used in Ayurveda. Small studies suggest it may help stress and sleep, and a few report small strength gains, but the evidence is limited. NCCIH notes possible side effects, including rare cases of liver injury, and that it should not be used in pregnancy; it can also interact with thyroid and other medicines. Talk to a doctor before taking it.

## Electrolytes and sweating
In hot, humid weather you lose water and salt in sweat. For most sessions, water and normal meals are enough. For long, very sweaty sessions (over about an hour), a drink with some salt and sugar, such as an oral rehydration solution or lemon water with a pinch of salt and sugar, helps replace losses.

## Multivitamins
Most people who eat a varied diet do not need a multivitamin. Some may need specific nutrients, for example vitamin B12 for strict vegetarians and vegans, or iron and vitamin D when a blood test shows they are low. Ask a doctor before taking high doses.

## Eating only protein to lose weight
A diet of only protein is hard to keep up and leaves out fibre, carbohydrate for training and important nutrients. Fat loss comes from a modest calorie deficit; keeping protein high (about 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg a day, as several sports-nutrition reviews suggest in a deficit) with strength training helps keep muscle. A balanced plate is easier and safer.
