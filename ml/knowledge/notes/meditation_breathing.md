## How to start meditating
Start small: 5 to 10 minutes a day, at the same time if you can. Sit comfortably on a chair or cushion, upright but relaxed, and rest your attention on your breathing. When the mind wanders, notice it and come back. Consistency matters more than length; add minutes once it feels easy. ASCEND has Mindfulness Meditation, Breath Counting and Body Scan to log.

## What to do when your mind keeps wandering
A wandering mind is normal, even for people who have meditated for years. The practice is the moment you notice you have drifted and gently return to the breath, without scolding yourself. Counting breaths from 1 to 10 gives the mind something to hold on to.

## Meditation posture
You do not need to sit cross-legged. A chair with the feet flat on the floor works well, as does kneeling on a cushion or lying down for a body scan. Keep the back upright but not stiff and the hands resting. If the legs go numb or the back aches, change position; pain is not part of the practice.

## What meditation can and cannot do
Research summarised by the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) suggests meditation and mindfulness practices may help with stress, anxiety and depression symptoms, and sleep for some people, though results vary and many studies are small. It is a support, not a treatment: anyone with a mental health condition should keep working with their doctor.

## Strong emotions during meditation
Meditation can sometimes bring up anger, sadness or anxiety. Open your eyes, return to normal breathing, take a short walk, or switch to a more active practice like walking meditation. NCCIH notes that meditation is generally considered safe for healthy people, but a few people report worse anxiety or depression. If difficult feelings keep coming or are intense, stop and talk to a doctor or counsellor.

## Best time to meditate
Any time you can keep is the best time. Mornings before the day gets busy work for many people; a short practice before bed can help you wind down. Meditating right after a heavy meal can make you sleepy.

## Breathing exercises for stress
Slow breathing, with a longer out-breath than in-breath, is a simple way to calm down: for example in for 4 and out for 6, for a few minutes. Box breathing and belly breathing work the same way. If you feel light-headed, go back to normal breathing. Sudden breathlessness or chest pain needs medical help, not a breathing exercise.

## Box breathing
Box breathing has four equal parts: breathe in for 4, hold for 4, breathe out for 4, hold for 4, repeated for a few minutes. Shorter counts are fine. It is a simple way to slow the breath before training, an exam or sleep. ASCEND has a Box Breathing entry.

## 4-7-8 breathing
In 4-7-8 breathing you breathe in through the nose for 4 counts, hold for 7, and breathe out through the mouth for 8, for about four rounds. Many people find it relaxing, though there is little research on this exact pattern. Skip the hold or shorten the counts if it makes you dizzy.

## Belly (diaphragmatic) breathing
Belly breathing means breathing slowly so that the belly rises more than the chest. Lie or sit with one hand on the chest and one on the belly to feel it. A few minutes a day helps you notice and slow your breathing. ASCEND has a Diaphragmatic Breathing entry.

## Alternate nostril breathing (anulom vilom, nadi shodhana)
Close one nostril with the thumb, breathe in through the other, switch, and breathe out; then breathe in on that side, switch, and breathe out on the first side. Keep the breaths slow and easy without holds as a beginner, for about 5 minutes. Skip it when your nose is blocked.

## Bhramari (humming bee breath)
In bhramari you breathe in through the nose and breathe out slowly while humming like a bee, with the eyes closed and the fingers resting on the ear flaps if you like. Five to ten rounds, then sit quietly. Many people use it to settle before sleep.

## Kapalbhati and other forceful breathing
Kapalbhati uses fast, forceful out-breaths. It can cause dizziness, and traditional guidance and teachers advise against it during pregnancy and for people with high blood pressure, heart disease, hernia, epilepsy or recent surgery. Learn it from a qualified teacher. Gentle practices like belly breathing and alternate nostril breathing are better starting points.

## Body scan meditation
In a body scan you move your attention slowly through the body, from the toes to the head, noticing sensations without trying to change them. It takes 10 to 20 minutes and is often done lying down, which makes it a good practice before sleep.

## Loving-kindness (metta) meditation
Loving-kindness meditation repeats kind wishes, such as 'may I be well, may I be at ease', first for yourself, then for someone close, someone neutral, someone difficult and finally everyone. If it feels forced, that is normal; the wish matters more than the feeling. Ten minutes is a good session.

## Walking meditation
Walking meditation uses slow walking as the anchor: feel each lift, move and placement of the foot on a short, safe path, turning at the ends. It suits people who find sitting still hard, and it is a good way back if sitting brings up strong emotions.

## Progressive muscle relaxation
Progressive muscle relaxation tenses one muscle group gently for about 5 seconds and then lets it relax, moving from the feet to the face. It helps you notice the difference between tension and relaxation and is often used before sleep. Skip any injured area.

## Meditation and sleep
A calming practice before bed, such as a body scan, slow breathing or progressive muscle relaxation, can make it easier to fall asleep. Keep the lights low and the phone away. Ongoing trouble sleeping for weeks is worth talking to a doctor about.

## Meditation and exercise performance
Some athletes use breathing and mindfulness practice to stay calm and focused under pressure and to recover between sessions. Evidence for direct performance gains is limited; think of it as a tool for focus and stress, alongside training, sleep and food.
