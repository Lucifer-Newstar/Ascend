## How much activity WHO recommends for adults
WHO's global recommendation for adults is at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity across the week. Moderate means your heart beats faster and you breathe harder than usual, like brisk walking. WHO also says muscle-strengthening activity benefits everyone, so add strength work (bodyweight, dumbbells, bands) to the walking, cycling or sport you already do.

## Any activity is better than none
The WHO 2020 guidelines carry four simple messages: any amount of physical activity is better than none, all physical activity counts (walking to class, stairs, chores, sport, gym), everyone should limit the time they spend sitting, and muscle strengthening benefits everyone. If 150 minutes a week feels far away, start with what you can do and build up.

## Sitting too much (sedentary time)
Sedentary behaviour is any time awake spent sitting, reclining or lying with very little energy use, such as long hours at a desk, on a phone or in a bus. WHO advises every age group to limit sedentary time and to replace some of it with activity of any intensity, even light movement like standing up and walking for a few minutes.

## Who is not active enough
WHO's fact sheet reports that about 31% of adults worldwide (around 1.8 billion people, 2022 data) do not reach 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and that about 80% of adolescents do not meet the recommended levels. Women are on average less active than men.

## Benefits of regular activity
Regular physical activity helps prevent and manage heart disease, some cancers and type 2 diabetes, reduces symptoms of depression and anxiety, supports brain health and improves overall well-being. In children and adolescents it supports bone health, healthy growth, muscle development and motor and cognitive development.

## Activity during pregnancy and after birth
WHO lists benefits of activity for pregnant and post-partum women, including lower risk of pre-eclampsia, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, excessive weight gain, delivery complications and postpartum depression, and says activity has no adverse effect on birth weight. Anyone pregnant should plan their exercise with their doctor or midwife, especially for new or intense training.
