## Flexibility or mobility
Flexibility is how far a muscle lets a joint move when you are relaxed (for example in a stretch); mobility is how well you control that range in movement, such as squatting deep with good form. Stretching helps the first; slow, controlled movement through the range (hip circles, Cossack squats, deep squats) helps the second. Most people benefit from a few minutes of both.

## How long to hold a stretch
Holding a static stretch for about 15 to 60 seconds, repeated two to four times, is a common recommendation; many people use 30 seconds. Stretch to mild tension, never sharp pain, and breathe slowly. Regular practice (most days) matters more than one long session. Save long static stretches for after training or a separate session.

## How to get the front splits
Front splits take most people several months of regular practice. Warm up, then use half-split (kneeling hamstring) stretches, deep lunges for the hip flexors, and supported split practice with blocks or cushions under the hips. Lower only to mild tension, keep the hips square, and never bounce or force the last part. ASCEND has a Front Split Practice entry.

## Middle splits (side splits)
Side splits depend partly on the shape of your hip joints, so some people never reach a flat middle split however much they stretch. Work on it safely with the pancake stretch, wide straddle stretches, horse stance and Cossack squats, and stop at any pinching in the hip or pain in the knee.

## Touching your toes
Being able to touch your toes is not a health test; people with stiff hamstrings can be perfectly fit. If you want it, regular forward-fold and hamstring stretches with bent knees at first will get you there over weeks. Hinge from the hips rather than rounding the back as hard as possible.

## Tight hip flexors from sitting
Long hours of sitting can leave the front of the hips feeling tight. Break up sitting every 30 to 60 minutes, and add hip flexor stretches such as the kneeling hip flexor stretch or the couch stretch, squeezing the glute of the back leg. Strengthening the glutes with bridges and lunges helps too.

## Desk posture habits
There is no single perfect posture; the best posture is the next one. Change position often, set the screen near eye level, keep the feet supported, and take short movement breaks. Rows, face pulls, band pull-aparts and open book rotations train the upper back. Pain that lasts or spreads into an arm or leg needs a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Upper back (thoracic) mobility
A stiff upper back can limit overhead pressing and handstands. Open book rotations, cat-cow, thread-the-needle stretches and foam rolling the upper back (not the lower back) help. Move slowly through the range with the breath, 8 to 10 reps.

## Ankle mobility for deeper squats
Stiff ankles push the heels up or the chest forward in a squat. The knee-to-wall drill, calf stretches and holding a deep squat for short periods help. Squatting with a small heel lift is fine while you work on it. ASCEND has a Knee-to-Wall Ankle Mobilisation entry.

## Wrist preparation for push-ups and handstands
Wrists take a lot of load in push-ups, planche work and handstands. Before training, spend 2 to 3 minutes on wrist circles and gentle rocking on hands and knees with the fingers pointing forward, sideways and back. Build handstand time gradually. Sharp or lasting wrist pain is a sign to stop and get it checked.

## Foam rolling
Foam rolling can make muscles feel looser and give a short-term increase in range of motion, which makes it a reasonable warm-up or cool-down. It does not break up scar tissue or remove toxins. Roll slowly over muscles, not joints or the lower spine, at a pressure that feels like a strong massage, not pain.

## Yoga for beginners
Start with simple poses: child's pose, cat-cow, downward dog, cobra, warrior II, tree pose and bridge pose, with slow breathing. A beginner class or a gentle guided video helps with alignment. Yoga is not a competition; use blocks or bent knees and skip any pose that causes pain. ASCEND has these poses to log.

## Yoga with strength training
Yoga and lifting work well together: yoga adds mobility, balance and a calm session, and lifting adds strength. Do gentle yoga on rest days or after training, and avoid long, deep stretching right before heavy lifting. Count a hard power-yoga class as a training day.

## Sun salutation
The sun salutation (surya namaskar) is a flowing sequence: reach up, fold forward, step back to plank, lower, lift into cobra, push back to downward dog, step forward and rise. Move with one breath per step. Three to six rounds make a good warm-up; knees down in the plank is fine.

## Stretching after a minor injury
After a strain or sprain, gentle movement within the pain-free range usually helps once the first painful days settle, but how soon and how much depends on the injury. A doctor or physiotherapist should guide it. Stretching hard into pain can make an injury worse.

## Stretching after eating
Gentle stretching or walking after a light meal is fine for most people. Deep forward folds, twists or inversions on a full stomach can feel uncomfortable, so many people wait one to two hours after a large meal before a full yoga session.

## Sitting on the floor comfortably
To sit cross-legged or on the floor for longer, sit on a firm cushion so the hips are higher than the knees, and change position often. Hip openers like the 90/90 hip switch and pigeon pose help over time. Do not force a lotus position: twisting the knee into it can injure it.

## Neck stiffness
Gentle neck movements (looking left and right, ear towards shoulder, chin tucks) and shoulder rolls can ease everyday stiffness, along with breaks from looking down at a phone or laptop. See a doctor quickly for neck pain after a fall or blow, or with numbness, tingling or weakness in the arms, headache, or fever.

## Legs up the wall on rest days
Lying with the legs resting up a wall for a few minutes is a calm, low-effort position many people use on rest days or before bed, with slow breathing. It is a relaxation practice, not a treatment. ASCEND has a Legs Up the Wall entry.
